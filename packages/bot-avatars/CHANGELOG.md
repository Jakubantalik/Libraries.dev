# Changelog

## 0.2.0

An inflated body for `plastic` and `fabric`. A toy is thickest in the middle and rounds off to nothing at its outline all round, like a cushion, so the body is no longer an outline pushed back into a slab: its slices through the depth follow the height field — each the outline scaled to the area and centre of where the surface stands at least that high — so the side silhouette is the cushion's own round profile. The slices carry no texture, only the side's light taken from the front's own texels where each slice's edge lies on it (so they match it exactly, on both halves, with no step at the equator), the far half sinking into shade toward its back; the lit front is laid over them once, stretched along the turn, fading out over its outer band into them, and cross-fading away when the body is turned far round, so a side view is one smooth rounded surface with no seam or ribbing. Plastic's thin environment line along the silhouette fades out as the head turns, so it no longer shows as a line across a turned body. The height field is puffed up further than before (it grows with the shape's inner width as well as the depth), which also rounds the shading. `roundness` (0–1, default 1) bends the profile toward a slab with soft edges. The bow tie sits on the body's surface where it is, and the face's clip is the outline a little over halfway up. About 0.2–0.4 ms of main thread a frame.

Studio light for `fabric`: a broad soft key from the upper left that turns gently into shade — a pile scatters light through itself, so the shade side stays lit, keeps the body's colour and deepens it (a yellow toward orange, not olive) rather than greying it — a fill from the other side, a back light the hairs catch round the silhouette and the floor's occlusion under the body. Fabric has its own default light: `shadow` 0.9, `highlight` 0.9, `light` 295, `rim` 0.65, `spread` 1.45 (plastic keeps 0.35, 1.3, 300, 0.5, 1.55); a prop given still wins.

The plush is a faux fur like a toy's. Its strands are a sparse bright noise drawn out along the fur's flow (a line-integral blur), combed from a parting behind the top of the head and falling, turning with slow swirls; they gather into locks — a coarse noise drawn out the same way — each a low mound lit on the side toward the light, its tip lying over the next one's root and shading it; a few longer guard hairs lie over them. The pile is kept as a film in two parts, black where it deepens the colour (multiplying, as occlusion does) and white where it lightens it (the tips, less on a dark colour so they stay a lighter shade of it), over a square wider than the body's, and laid over the whole body in one pass — front, sides and back as it turns — so it is one continuous pile with no ring at any slice. The silhouette breaks into soft tufts where the locks stand past it, with a haze of fine hairs rooted along it. On a plush body the bow tie is the same felt, grain and fuzz round each wing, instead of satin.

The pile has a style: `furLength` (default 1.4: the strands, the locks and the tufts), `furDensity` (1.6), `furFuzz` (0.9: the tufts and the haze at the silhouette), `furClumps` (0.35: how strongly the locks show), `furCurl` (0.3: how much the flow waves) and `furGravity` (0.6: the pile combed down from the parting and hanging off the sides and bottom, with a few flyaways so it is not the same all round), each baked once per shape on idle time in steps of a few milliseconds and shared; while a slider is dragged only the two newest bakes for a shape are kept in the queue.

A `fabric` shading: the same baked form in a plush faux fur (above). The creases take a soft occlusion, and the form's baked edge darkening is taken back out, exactly, since fur catches the light at the silhouette instead. The pile belongs to the shape: one per outline, depth, light direction, style and resolution tier (192, 320 or 512 texels, picked by how large the avatar is drawn), shared by every avatar of that type and baked on idle time. The idle queue drains as much work as each idle period allows instead of one task per callback.

Things to wear: `hat` (`beret`, `beanie`, `party`, `crown`), `glasses` (`round`, `square`, `shades`), `headphones` and `bowTie`, with `accessoryColor`. Each is a small 3D object built from lit surfaces: a pleated felt beret with a leather binding and a stalk; a beanie knitted stitch by stitch, with a ribbed cuff and a yarn pompom; a paper party hat with stripes wrapping the cone, a foil trim and tinsel; a gold crown with ridged points, pearls on their tips, cut stones in gold settings, gold buds and studs, a puffed velvet cap and an orb and cross; over-ear headphones with creased leather cushions, rounded shells, brushed metal rings round gloss plates, yokes that pivot and a padded, stitched band that rests on the head; acetate glasses with rounded rims, rivets and lenses that reflect; a satin bow tie with folds gathered into its knot. Each sits on the body's own landmarks, turns with the head, is lit from the body's light and casts a soft contact shadow; the far headphone cup and the band's far end go behind the head as it turns away, and the glasses leave with the eyes on a wide turn. They are drawn by a small surface renderer (`mesh.ts`, canvas 2D): every surface is a grid built once per shape and size and only projected each frame, painted a strip at a time — the visible stretch of each row one shape, filled with a gradient that carries the light along it from the corners' own normals — back to front across the object, with cut facets a cell at a time and pearls and rivets as single radial fills; grids are sized from how large the object is on screen. About 0.2–0.8 ms a frame at 120 px.

## 0.1.0

First release: eighteen types (clover, flower, triangle, square, blob, ghost, circle, drop, star, droid, mech, alien, hexagon, cat, cloud, pill, pebble, puddle), two faces (eyes, mouth), three states (default, working, sleeping) with head turns, hops, flips and cross-animated switches, palette colours with `color`, `brightness`, `saturation` and `ink` overrides, `size`, `speed`, `paused`, `seed`, `shading` (per-pixel plastic by default, crisp, smooth or flat) with `shadow`, `highlight`, `light`, `depth`, `rim` and `spread`. Eyes and head follow the pointer, a click hops and flips (`interactive`). The face is clipped to the front of the body, so a wide turn can never carry a feature off the silhouette. Settling back to idle from working takes a second and a fifth,, and a hop under way flies its whole arc, finishes its turn and lands with a jump's proper landing — the hold on the ground and the rise back — before the hops stop; every state switch eases on a sine. Idle, the head looks to a corner, stays a few seconds and then swings across to the opposite one — fifty degrees of turn either side, fourteen of rise and a slight tilt with it — the eyes running ahead of the turn (`turn` scales how far it goes to the side), and they draw taller looking up and shorter looking down. Rendered on a 2D canvas as a lit extrusion; the rig and renderer are exported.

Performance pass before release: the plastic material rebuilds its lighting only once the light or view has moved a whole bin (numeric keys, no strings per frame), keeps its texels in canvases of its own (two in turn, which also fixes Safari showing another avatar's texture: WebKit reads a `drawImage` source when the frame flushes), picks the texture tier from the real device size (the old read of the context transform included the squash, so tiers flipped mid-hop and small avatars got the coarse tier), bakes forms one per idle slot (Safari has no `requestIdleCallback`; before, every type on a page baked in one go), and every mode sets slice transforms relative to the previous slice instead of save/transform/translate/restore. Crisp and smooth precompute their slice colours per palette; the theme is resolved per render, not per frame. `DrawConfig.dpr` tells the renderer the context's scale so it never reads the transform back. Eyes are stroked as short polylines (Safari drew the open eye's curve as a teardrop). The whirl is off by default; `whirl={1}` turns it on. On WebKit (Safari, every iOS browser) the plastic side slices are blitted from three sprites of the outline, redrawn only when the lighting rebuilds, because a conic-gradient fill there costs thirty times a flat one: a page of eighteen avatars went from about 19 ms to 1.6 ms of frame time in Safari, and the only difference from the vector path is a pixel of anti-aliasing along the side stack's silhouette. Chromium and Firefox keep filling the slices as vectors; `DrawConfig.sides` forces either path. The idle flip (and a click's) is the working state's spinning hop on its own: the same sine arc, timing, stretch at the top, lean and shut eyes, with a crouch before take-off; its landing squash starts at the moment of contact and recovers without a wobble. Leaving the working state, the hop under way finishes whole and its landing fades, instead of hops shrinking with the state weight and a spin ending short of a turn. The lighting cross-fades between rebuilds, so a slow turn no longer steps a bin at a time. The canvas overscans 1.5× with the body a tenth of the box below its middle, so a spin hop is not cropped at the top. The mouth is one shape whose numbers blend across states, a morph instead of a cross-fade of three. `saturation` defaults to 1.5. The jump's numbers are props — `jumpHeight`, `jumpTime`, `jumpStretch`, `jumpSpin`, `jumpLean`, `jumpEvery`, `jumpLand` (when the landing squash begins, round the moment of contact), `jumpSquash`, `jumpSquashTime` and `jumpSquashEase` (how deep, how long and in what shape: sharp, pulse, soft or bouncy), `jumpGroundTime` and `jumpGroundEase` (how long the deepest squash is held on the ground, and how the weight settles through it), and `jumpRiseTime` / `jumpRiseEase` for the way back from the deepest squash to the body's own shape — a landing is the press, the hold and the rise, each with its own time and easing; squash and stretch scale about the body's base, so a landing keeps the feet on the ground. The breathing cycle integrates its phase, so its period can slow into sleep without the body pulsing through the switch. A click's jump lands with a squash half as long again as an idle jump's (`jumpClickSquashTime`, 0.24 s, a Studio slider) — with a Studio group for them. The head's idle wander moves as a lightly damped spring instead of an exponential approach, so each turn starts and ends softly.

A React Native port lives at `ports/react-native` (`bot-avatars-native`, local-only, plus an Expo example): the same rig imported from this package, drawn with react-native-skia and Reanimated — the slice stack, the face, the whirl, and the plastic material baked with `bakeBotAvatarForm` and lit per pixel in an SkSL port of `buildMatcap`.

A SwiftUI port for iOS 17+ lives at `ports/ios` (BotAvatarsKit, a SwiftPM package, and the BotAvatarsDemo app): the same rig, renderer and plastic material drawn into a `Canvas`, with a tap for the click and a finger drag for the pointer, plus `BotAvatarStage`, one canvas for a whole roster.
