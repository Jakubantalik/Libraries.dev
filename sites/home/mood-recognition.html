Mood recognitionBeta

Interfaces that read the user's mood. One small on-device model reads frustration, confusion, or when someone is lost, from how they move the pointer, press, scroll and type, and tells your UI, so the screen can bring forward what helps. No LLM, no server, nothing leaves the page.

The model

One model reads how someone is using the page, four times a second, and tells your UI how they are doing: frustrated, confused, lost, or fine. It is input only: pair it with any component, and let the screen bring forward what helps.

MoodRecognition.json · v0.4 beta · 142 KB (32 KB gzipped) · React kit · Model License

Moods

  • Frustrated Fighting the interface: pressing a locked or disabled control, quick repeated presses on one spot or on nothing, the pointer shaken back and forth, a field typed and sent the same way after it failed. A blocked step reads as frustrated.
  • Confused Unsure what to do next, read from how the pointer moves alone, its speed and its path: circling one spot or hovering round a control without pressing it, slowing down and turning back.
  • Lost Looking for something: returning to the same few places without pressing, opening section after section, scrolling back and forth, a long time since any progress.
  • Away Not looking: the tab hidden, the pointer out of the window, or nothing moving for a while. A plain rule rather than the model, so the screen can stay as it is.

How someone is doing

  • Reads 43 measures of the last 8 seconds, some looking back 15, 30 or 60: how the pointer moves (speed, wiggle, shaking, circling one spot, returning to the same places without pressing), presses (repeated on one spot, on nothing, on disabled controls, near misses), scrolling back and forth, typing and deleting, invalid entries, and a field typed and sent the same way after it failed.
  • Answers How likely each reading is: fine, blocked, lost, confused, waiting or frustrated, and one score for all of them together. A new answer every 250 ms.
  • Tested On real sessions from more than 10 people with planted problems (a Pay that stays locked, a strict date format, a buried setting, a silent slow save), each scored by a model that had never seen them: 48 of 60 problems caught at the alert line, and 2.3 % of calm time flagged (median person 1.5 %).

The obvious cases, at once

  • Reads The last second or two: the pointer shaken back and forth at speed, circling one spot, three quick presses on one spot or on no control, a press on a disabled control.
  • Answers Frustrated, or confused for circling, within a few hundred milliseconds: what the 8 second model is slow to see.

A reaction that never flickers

  • Reads The model and the fast signals together, every 66 ms.
  • Answers One number for your UI, 0 to 1. It starts at 0.30 of the reading and is at full strength at the 0.65 alert line. It rises within about a tenth of a second and, once calm, holds a second, then eases back over a few seconds, or about half a minute while the pointer rests: someone stuck may be reading.

Inside: 360 small gradient-boosted trees in plain JSON. One call is a few hundred comparisons, with no dependencies.

Built for the web

Trained on simulated and real sessions

The model learned from 80 simulated people with planted episodes (shaking, circling, revisiting, retrying) and from real usage: sessions of more than 10 people on mouse, trackpad and touch, which teach it what everyday use looks like, so it stays quiet when nothing is wrong. No user data.

Beta

Known weak spots: a short lost search, under about 20 seconds, can stay just under the line; a blocked checkout is often read as lost; someone exploring many sections of an easy flow can read as lost. More people, devices and kinds of screens are being recorded.

In the browser, four times a second

142 KB of trees, 32 KB gzipped, bundled with your app. No LLM, no server, no network request at runtime.

Nothing recorded

It reads positions, timings and facts about the elements under the pointer (a button, disabled, invalid), never text, field values or the screen. Nothing leaves the page, and no permission prompt.

Pair it with any component

The mood and the reaction are plain values: dim the locked Pay and lift the terms that unlock it, as above, show a hint, open a way out, or slow an animation down. React 18 or newer, any modern browser.

Usage 
import { useState } from "react";
import { MoodProvider, useMood } from "./mood-recognition";
import model from "./mood-recognition/MoodRecognition.json";

// One provider per page; the components that adapt sit inside it.
export function Checkout() {
  return (
    <MoodProvider model={model}>
      <PayForm />
    </MoodProvider>
  );
}

function PayForm() {
  // mood: "frustrated" | "confused" | "lost" | "away" | null
  const { mood } = useMood();
  const [agreed, setAgreed] = useState(false);
  // 1 neutral, 2 confused or lost, 3 frustrated; agreed, nothing is locked
  const state = agreed ? 1 : mood === "frustrated" ? 3 : mood === "confused" || mood === "lost" ? 2 : 1;

  return (
    <form data-state={state}>
      <button disabled={!agreed}>Pay $97.54</button>
      <label className="terms">
        <input type="checkbox" checked={agreed} onChange={(e) => setAgreed(e.target.checked)} />
        I agree to the terms of sale and the return policy
      </label>
    </form>
  );
}
Style each state 
/* Confused or lost: the locked Pay steps back, so it stops looking like the next step. */
form[data-state="2"] button:disabled,
form[data-state="3"] button:disabled { opacity: 0.2; }
/* Frustrated: the terms, the one thing that unlocks Pay, come forward too. */
form[data-state="3"] .terms { color: #e8e8e8; }

/* Or blend a calm and an emphatic style by the reaction, 0 … 1,
   which the provider sets as --mood for everything inside it. */
.hint { opacity: var(--mood, 0); }
Each reading
FieldWhat it is
mood"frustrated", "confused", "lost" or "away"; null while calm or without input. Blocked reads as frustrated. A mood holds for a second before it fades.
reactionHow strongly the UI should react, 0 … 1: from 0.30 of the reading, full at the 0.65 alert line. Rises in about 0.1 s, eases back over a few seconds once calm. Away doesn't raise it. Also set as --mood for the CSS inside the provider.
scoreThe strength behind the mood, 0 … 1: the higher of the model and the fast signals.
probabilitiesThe model's belief in each reading: none, blocked, lost, confused, waiting and frustrated, summing to 1; null without input or a model.
fastThe fast signals, 0 … 1, and fastKind: "shake", "orbit", "rage" or "disabled"; null when none.
isModelLoadedWhether the model passed in could be read. Without it, only the fast signals and away work.
Good to know
  • It reads the whole page, as the model was trained, wherever the provider sits. Mark parts it should skip, such as your own help panel, with data-mood-ignore. Use one provider per page.
  • It reads positions, timings and element facts (a button, disabled, invalid), never text, field values or the screen. Nothing leaves the page.
  • Everyone moves at their own pace. It starts from typical values and learns the person's own pointer speed and presses as they go, kept in localStorage; baselineKey={null} keeps it in memory only. reactTo picks the moods the reaction rises for (frustrated, confused and lost by default).
  • React to frustrated, confused and lost, not to away: someone away isn't looking. Keep the reaction small and reversible: bring forward what unlocks, a hint or a way out, never block or move things from under the pointer.
  • The model reads an 8 second window, so slow patterns, like getting lost, take a few seconds to show. The fast signals see shaking and repeated presses within a few hundred milliseconds.
  • Without React: createMoodReader({ model, scope, onReading }).start().
  • Beta: trained on simulated people and real sessions of more than 10 people, on mouse, trackpad and touch. Short lost searches can stay under the line, and a blocked checkout is often read as lost.

Run it (TypeScript) 
import model from "./MoodRecognition.json";

// Each tree adds to one class; a softmax of the sums gives the probabilities.
function predict(x: number[]) {
  const sums = new Array(model.classes.length).fill(0);
  for (const t of model.trees) {
    let i = 0;
    while (t.f[i] >= 0) {
      const v = x[t.f[i]];
      i = Number.isNaN(v) ? (t.m[i] ? t.l[i] : t.r[i]) : v <= t.t[i] ? t.l[i] : t.r[i];
    }
    sums[t.c] += t.v[i];
  }
  const top = Math.max(...sums);
  const e = sums.map((s) => Math.exp(s - top));
  const total = e.reduce((a, b) => a + b, 0);
  const probs = e.map((v) => v / total);
  return { probs, score: 1 - probs[model.classes.indexOf("none")] };
}

// x: model.features in order, measured over the last model.windowMs (8 s).
The file
  • MoodRecognition.json Plain JSON, format "fx-gbdt-1": name, version, data (what it was trained on), trained, features (43 names, in order), classes (none, blocked, lost, confused, waiting, frustrated), windowMs (8,000), stepMs (250) and trees.
  • trees 360 trees, as flat arrays: c the class it adds to, and per node f the feature (−1 for a leaf), t the threshold (left when the value is at most it), l and r the children, v the leaf value and m where a missing value goes (1 left).
  • features Pointer (speed, wiggle, straightness, jerk, shaking, circling, revisits), presses (repeated, on nothing, on disabled controls, near misses), scrolling, keys and deletes, invalid entries, retyping, time since progress, and the person's own pace against their baseline.
How each feature is measured is in the React kit's features.ts, the single source for training and runtime. Beta: trained on simulated people and real sessions of more than 10 people, and tested on each person with a model that had never seen them.

Add Mood recognition (beta) from Libraries.dev to my React app: a UI that notices when
the user is frustrated, confused or lost, read on-device from how the pointer,
presses, scrolling and typing behave, and adapts the screen to help.

Install: the React kit and the model are downloads, not npm packages. Ask the
user to download both from https://libraries.dev/mood-recognition (Install tab, free
account): MoodRecognition-React-0.4.zip (the kit's TypeScript source, MIT) unzipped
into src/mood-recognition/, and MoodRecognition-0.4.zip (MoodRecognition.json) placed next to
it. Import the model so it ships inside the bundle (needs resolveJsonModule).
React 18+, no other deps.

Usage:
import { MoodProvider, useMood } from "./mood-recognition";
import model from "./mood-recognition/MoodRecognition.json";
<MoodProvider model={model}>...</MoodProvider>   // one per page; reads the whole page
const { mood, reaction } = useMood();
Props: model, baselineKey (default "mood-baseline"; null keeps the person's
pace in memory), reactTo (default ["frustrated", "confused", "lost"]).
Without React: createMoodReader({ model, scope, onReading }).start().

Moods:
- frustrated: fighting the interface (pressing a locked or disabled control,
  rapid presses on one spot or on nothing, shaking the pointer, retyping what
  failed); a blocked step reads as frustrated
- confused: unsure what to do next, from the pointer's speed and path alone
  (circling one spot, hovering round a control without pressing it, slowing
  down and turning back)
- lost: looking for something (returning to the same places without pressing,
  opening section after section, scrolling back and forth)
- away: not looking (tab hidden, pointer out of the window, nothing moving);
  a plain rule, don't react to it

Readings (update about every 66 ms):
- mood: one of the above or null (null while calm or without input; a mood
  holds 1 s before fading)
- reaction: 0-1, how strongly the UI should react: from 0.30 of the reading,
  full at the 0.65 alert line; rises in ~0.1 s, eases back over a few seconds
  once calm (about half a minute while the pointer rests); away doesn't raise
  it. The provider also sets it as --mood for the CSS inside it.
- score: 0-1, the strength behind the mood (higher of model and fast signals)
- probabilities: none, blocked, lost, confused, waiting, frustrated (sum to 1),
  or null without input or a model
- fast / fastKind: instant signals 0-1, "shake" | "orbit" | "rage" | "disabled"
- isModelLoaded
data-mood-ignore on an element skips it (e.g. your own help panel).

How to adapt: keep it small and reversible: bring forward what unlocks the
blocked step, show a hint or a way out; never block or move things from under
the pointer. A pattern with three states, for a Pay locked behind a terms box:
state = agreed ? 1 : mood === "frustrated" ? 3 : mood === "confused" || mood === "lost" ? 2 : 1

unlock it also brighten. Or blend styles by var(--mood, 0).
